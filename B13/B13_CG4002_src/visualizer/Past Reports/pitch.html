CG4002 · Team Pitch · Group Discussion

Split
Ring

Two-player AR boxing. Each fighter stands in their own square and sees the other rendered into it — so nobody ever gets hit, and the system has to infer every punch that lands.

Working title. Change it if you hate it.

What I need from you tomorrow

Seven calls. Five are mine to propose and yours to veto; one costs money, and one nobody has a number for yet.

  1. Separated rings — do we accept that players never touch?
  2. The arm chain — do we spend ~$80 on 6 more IMUs? Costs money.
  3. Classification, not collision — how a hit is decided.
  4. The telegraph — an on-screen warning that a punch is coming. New.
  5. Items and gestures — the pickup mechanic.
  6. Pose stream — Comms and Visualizer take a new data path.
  7. Round length — nobody has picked a number yet.

What a match actually looks like

Before the bell
Both players strap on arm sensors and head-mount their phone. The lobby runs a 30-second calibration — hold your guard, throw one of each punch. This is not decoration: it is what makes the system work on a person it has never seen.
The round
You are alone in your square. Your opponent appears in front of you, rendered into your real room, moving as they really move. You throw real punches at them. They are three metres and one wall away, so nobody gets hurt. When they start to throw something, a marker flashes on the side it is coming from — the system calls the punch before it finishes.
Between exchanges
Crates spawn on your floor. Only you can see yours. Reaching for one drops your guard for about half a second — which is exactly when you eat a hook.
The bell
Health hits zero, or the timer runs out and whoever has more health wins.

Decision 1 · Proposed

The move that makes this buildable

Putting each player in their own square is the one decision everything else hangs off. It solves three problems at once that we had no good answer to:

ProblemHow separated rings solve it
Safety A phone strapped to your face means you cannot take head contact. Separate squares make that structural instead of relying on players holding back.
Knowing who is in range This was blocking everything. With separate rings, engagement distance becomes a number the game defines rather than a measurement the hardware must make.
Requirement 2 Nothing on the floor, nothing in the room, no infrastructure sensors. Just space.

The cost: because the opponent is never physically there, we have to synthesise them entirely. That is the next two sections, and it is where the real engineering is.

Decision 2 · Open · Costs money

What your opponent will look like

With one IMU on each forearm plus the phone on your head, we know three things about the opponent's body: where their head is, and which way each forearm is pointing. We do not know where their hands are in space, and we know nothing at all about their torso, hips or legs.

An IMU measures rotation well and position badly — working out position means integrating acceleration twice, which drifts into nonsense within seconds. So with today's sensor list, the honest render is two gloves and a head, floating. Plenty of VR boxing games ship exactly this and it looks fine. But we should choose it now, not discover it in Week 10.

We can do better, and it is cheaper than you'd think

Full-body IMU motion capture is a solved commercial product — Xsens, Rokoko and Perception Neuron all do it with 17–19 sensors and no cameras. The trick is that they never integrate acceleration. They put a sensor on each rigid body segment, take each segment's orientation, assume bone lengths from a calibration pose, and derive every joint position by walking the skeleton from a root outwards.

Twenty nodes is obviously out on both budget and Beetle count. But Requirement 3 says the system must run on the FireBeetle — not that every sensor needs its own. One Beetle per player with several IMUs on an I2C mux changes the maths completely.

OptionRough costWhat we get

B — Arm chain

C — Full body
~10 segments per player		 $400+ Dead. Over budget, and far past "a few Beetles per team".

Three things to weigh before we buy anything

  • Drift is the real enemy, not sensor count. Gravity pins down tilt, but nothing pins down which way a segment is facing without a magnetometer — and indoor magnetometers get wrecked by steel and electronics. Over a three-minute round the avatar slowly unwinds. There is a free fix: reference the chest sensor's heading to the phone's own tracking, which is already world-locked.
  • It helps the AI too — more than I first realised. Upper-arm rotation is exactly what separates a hook from a cross, and it roughly triples the size of the input the network sees, which makes the FPGA work a lot easier to justify. It is also the difference between being able to call a punch while it is being thrown and not. See Decision 4 — that mechanic mostly stands or falls on this purchase.
  • It weakens my own argument in the next section. Good glove positions make collision detection tempting again. I still think classification wins, but I would be defending a different position than the one below.

The cost lands on Sensors: a wired harness on someone throwing punches is a comfort, weight, snag and durability problem — and comfort and weight are named explicitly in how we get graded on design.

Decision 3 · Proposed

How we decide a punch landed

Once the opponent is a rendered figure with a hitbox, the obvious move is to check whether your glove is inside their head. I want us to deliberately not do that.

Two reasons. The practical one: we do not know where your glove is to the few centimetres that a head-versus-shoulder call needs — even with option B above. The strategic one: if combat is geometry, then combat is trigonometry the game engine does alone, and the entire AI subsystem becomes a thing that prints punch names nobody reads.

So a hit is a fusion of four things:

IngredientComes from
What punch was thrown, and how hardThe neural network, on the FPGA
Whether you were close enoughEngagement distance, from both players' movement
Whether they were guarding that sideThe neural network, on their arms
What that does to health and staminaGame engine

The avatar's gloves are for looking at. They never decide anything. That one sentence is what keeps the FPGA in the critical path rather than making it a decoration — so if you disagree with it, tomorrow is the time to say so.

Distance, without the two squares fighting each other

If both players step forward at once, a naive shared coordinate system puts them inside each other. So we never share one. Your opponent is always rendered facing you, and the gap between you is worked out from how far each of you has moved from your own starting spot, clamped so it can never go below arm's length. Step in and the distance closes; step out and it opens. Two players who both charge will feel the distance stop responding — that is the price, and I think it is worth paying.

Decision 4 · Proposed · New

Seeing the punch before it arrives

As designed so far, the defender has no information. A glove moves, and a moment later your health bar drops. Real boxing is not that — it is almost entirely reading what the other person is about to do. So: can the AI call the punch while it is still being thrown, and put a warning on the defender's screen?

Yes, and it is nearly free. It is the same network with one extra output, trained on the first fraction of a punch instead of the whole thing. I had already flagged early classification as our strongest novelty angle — this is what it looks like as a game mechanic rather than a line in a report.

But do the arithmetic before designing around it

A punch takes roughly a quarter of a second from the shoulder starting to move to the fist arriving. We need enough signal to guess, then the guess has to travel across BLE, the laptop, the Ultra96, back out to the other player's phone, and get drawn. That eats most of the quarter second.

Two things fall out of that picture, and they are the whole argument:

1. This is the real reason to buy the arm chain

A punch travels outwards through the body — hips, then trunk, then shoulder, then the elbow straightens. A forearm sensor is watching the last link in that chain. A sensor on the chest and upper arm sees the punch start 50–100 ms earlier, which roughly triples the warning we can give.

So option B is not just about the avatar looking better. It is what makes this mechanic work at all. If we say no to the arm chain, we should probably say no to the telegraph too — 35 ms is not a warning, it is a flicker.

2. It cannot be "dodge this punch"

Even at 105 ms, you cannot see it and then react — human reflex is about twice that. Anyone who designs a slip-the-hook mechanic on top of this will build something that feels broken. What the warning is genuinely good for is which guard you are already holding, which is exactly the stance-not-reaction decision we already made for blocking. You read the pattern over an exchange rather than flinching at one punch.

If we want it to bite harder, the game engine can also resolve the hit a fixed delay after the telegraph rather than the instant the punch finishes — every fighting game ever made clocks its wind-ups this way. That is a game-engine call, not an AI one.

What the indicator looks like

It has to be readable in a fraction of a second, in peripheral vision, while someone is punching. So: no text, no numbers, no health-bar-style readout. Shape says what is coming, position says where from, and it fades in and out over about 200 ms.

What it can and cannot tell you that early

At the moment we have to commitCan we call it?
Straight versus hookingYes — a hook has rotation in it from the very first frame; a straight does not.
Rising (uppercut) versus levelYes — it pushes against gravity in a way nothing else does.
Which arm it is coming fromFree — we know which sensor sent it.
Jab versus crossNo, not this early. Same motion, and the difference is mostly which arm anyway.
How hard it will landNo. The punch has not peaked yet.
Head or bodyNo.

The nice side effect: feints become real

Once a warning exists, starting a hook and aborting it makes your opponent commit their guard to nothing. That is authentic boxing, it balances out the advantage the telegraph hands the defender, and it costs us zero to implement — a feint is just a punch that never finishes, so the system produces it for free.

The failure mode I am worried about

A warning that cries wolf is worse than no warning. If it fires when you adjust your guard or reach for a crate and no punch follows, players will learn to ignore it inside one round and we will have built nothing.

So the bar for showing it is higher than the bar for classifying a completed punch, and "false warnings per minute" becomes a number I have to report alongside accuracy — not a thing I get to leave out.

The other cost is unglamorous: to train this, I need training data labelled with when each punch started, not just what it was. That is more careful annotation work than we were planning on, and it is the part of this most likely to bite.

What it asks of each of us

WhoWhat changes

And it quietly fixes my weakest argument. To catch a punch at its onset I have to run the network far more often than I do to classify a finished one — every 10–20 ms instead of every 40. That is four to eight times the inference load, and it comes from a gameplay requirement rather than from me inflating numbers to justify the FPGA. See the honest caveat at the end.

Decision 5 · Proposed

Items, and the one thing we have to change

Crates spawn on your own floor, invisible to your opponent. Three contents: thorn armour (attacking you hurts them), bandages (heal), poison (damage over time).

Crates are unmarked, and reaching for one is dangerous

You do not know what a crate holds until you pick it up. Without that, nobody ever picks up poison and it stops being a mechanic. And picking up means extending your arm, which drops your guard for about 600 ms. So every crate is a gamble twice over: what is in it, and whether you get countered for reaching. The gesture is the mechanic, not just an input method.

Open palm cannot work. It has to change.

A sensor on your forearm cannot tell whether your hand is open or in a fist — fingers and wrist don't move the forearm, so there is literally no signal to read. This is not a difficulty; it is an absence.

Proposed swap: reach out and turn your palm up. That is a forearm roll, which the gyroscope sees unambiguously. Same instinct — palm up to receive — and it actually works.

Same test applies to the three item-use gestures. Anything defined by hand shape, grip or finger position is invisible to us.

Using items

Three gestures, all picked to be things a boxer never does by accident, and all separable from punches by direction rather than by how hard they are:

GestureWhy the sensor can see it
Raise a forearm overheadGravity flips upside down. Impossible to confuse.
Press down toward the floorRolls the opposite way to the pickup gesture.
Tap your own chest twiceMoves inward, where every punch moves outward.

Your randomised mapping stays — it is doing real work

Which gesture triggers which item is shuffled every round. I originally wanted this off by default and I was wrong. Here is why it matters: the avatar renders your arm motion faithfully. With a fixed mapping, your opponent watches you raise an arm overhead and now knows you have thorn armour and should stop punching. Shuffling closes exactly that leak.

It costs the AI nothing — we recognise the gesture, the game engine decides what it means. And it is only hidden from your opponent: you are shown your own mapping. Put it as a small icon on the glove rather than a corner readout and the mid-fight glance mostly disappears.

Stamina

Punching costs stamina, scaled by how hard you threw it. Standing idle recovers fast but leaves you open; holding your guard recovers slowly but protects you. Below a floor, your punches lose their power.

Guard is a stance you hold, not a reaction. Human reaction time is around 200 ms and the opponent's motion reaches your screen in 60–110 ms — reactive blocking would be a coin flip. Held-stance blocking degrades gracefully instead of feeling broken.

Decision 6 · Affects Comms and Visualizer

How the pieces connect

There are two data paths, and keeping them separate matters.

Inside the Ultra96

The FPGA holds the trained network and nothing else. Everything that changes per player — calibration especially — happens on the ARM processor before data reaches the chip. We cannot re-burn a bitstream in the lobby, so personalisation has to live upstream. Getting this boundary wrong means rebuilding in Week 9, which is why it is written down now.

What this asks of each of us

Hardware Sensors

Four arm nodes, mounted to survive repeated impact. Possibly six more IMUs on a wired harness if we take option B.

Decide: arm chain, yes or no? And confirm how many Beetles we actually get.

Comms

The usual Beetle→laptop→Ultra96 path, plus a continuous pose stream between two phones that has a tighter latency budget than the combat path does.

Decide: does the broker carry Path B, and can it hold ~100 ms?

Software / Hardware AI — mine

Ten gesture classes plus punch strength, running continuously on four sensor streams, accelerated on the FPGA. Calibration, and the model that has to work on a stranger. Plus the early-warning head, and the data labelling it needs.

I own the contract on both ends. I need the formats frozen well before Week 7.

Software Visualizer

Avatar rendering and skeleton solving, world anchoring, crates, inventory, health and stamina, the game engine, and consuming the pose stream. The attack indicator is three shapes and a fade — the one cheap thing on this list.

This is the biggest single workload in the project. Say now if it is too much.

My honest read on the biggest risk

It is not the AI and it is not the FPGA. It is that this design quietly loads an enormous amount onto the Visualizer, and if that is not working by Week 9, my subsystem has nothing to talk to and yours has nothing to show. I would rather cut features tomorrow than find this out in October.

How we get there

By Week 7

Every subsystem works on fake data. For me: the whole pipeline in software, six punch classes, no FPGA yet. Plus a dumb threshold-based baseline — cheap to build, and beating it on players the model has never seen is our proof the AI earns its place.

Weeks 7–9

The FPGA route: C simulation, synthesis, co-simulation, then the block diagram and data plumbing. The plumbing is where projects stall, not the network. Ten classes. First real match. The early-warning head goes in here, once there is enough onset-labelled data to train it on.

Week 9 or 10

Integration check with a mandatory demo. Which week is genuinely unclear — our own sources disagree, so someone should ask the TA.

Week 12

Fully integrated, tested with a random player. Not graded, but it is the honest test of whether "works on anyone" is true.

Week 13

Final demo — 30% of the grade — plus poster and report.

Where I am not confident

Worth saying plainly, so nobody builds on top of something soft.

Open Team + TA

How long is a round?

I do not have a number and I am not going to invent one. Round length should fall out of the health pool divided by expected damage per second, with the timer as a backstop against a stalemate. Two sanity checks on whatever we land on: real boxing rounds are about three minutes with rest in between, and the final demo needs to run long enough for an evaluator to see every mechanic at least once. Nothing in the module materials says how long a demo slot is — worth asking rather than guessing.

Unproven Mine

The gestures are reasoned, not measured

The three item gestures are picked from physical reasoning about what a forearm sensor can distinguish. That reasoning is sound but nobody has recorded real data yet. If one turns out to collide with a punch, we swap it. Low risk, but not zero.

Honest caveat Mine

The FPGA is not strictly necessary at this scale

Running the numbers, the network is small enough that the ARM core on the Ultra96 could probably handle it. I would rather we say that out loud than get asked in the viva. The real arguments are predictable timing under load, keeping the processor free for everything else, energy per inference, and room to grow. Option B in the avatar section makes this meaningfully stronger, because it roughly triples the input size — and the telegraph makes it stronger again, because catching a punch at its onset means running the network four to eight times as often. Both of those come from product decisions rather than from me padding the numbers, which is the only kind of justification that survives being questioned.

Unproven Mine

Whether the early warning is accurate enough to be worth showing

The physics says a hook is distinguishable from a straight within the first few hundredths of a second, and I believe that. What I cannot promise yet is the false-alarm rate, and that is the number that decides whether the mechanic is fun or noise. If it cries wolf, we cut it — the rest of the design does not depend on it. It also needs training data annotated with punch onsets, which is more labelling work than we had planned for.

Settled Mine

Timing numbers are estimates

Every millisecond figure here is a target to measure against, not a measurement. They are the right order of magnitude and the shape of the budget is right — inference is the cheapest part, waiting for enough of the punch to have happened is the expensive part.

Safety, because the report requires it in writing

Separated rings remove the risk of hitting each other. They do not remove walls, furniture, trip hazards or bystanders — and every player has a phone strapped over their eyes.

  • A minimum clear square of about 2.5 × 2.5 m each, floor cleared first.
  • Draw the ring boundary in AR and warn when someone nears the edge. Cheap to build, and it is a safety control and a feature at the same time.
  • Someone spotting whenever an outsider plays, including the Week 12 test.
  • Test that the phone mount actually holds during full-speed punching before a stranger wears it.
  • Strap checks, no jewellery, capped session length.
